Reading the odds on your own dashboard

The three big numbers in your match centre are decimal odds. Decimal odds are the total return per 1 unit staked — stake 1 at 42.0, get 42 back (41 profit) if it wins. In fractional terms that's 41-to-1.

Convert to probability by inverting: implied probability = 1 ÷ decimal odds. Convert back with fair odds = 1 ÷ probability.

The tell Because the three implied probabilities sum to 100%, these are fair odds — no margin. A real sportsbook's prices always sum to more than 100%, and the excess is their profit. Your dashboard shows the honest probabilities; the pipeline publishes the bookmaker version alongside them.

02

TRADE360 — and what your pipeline mirrors

What LSports actually sells

LSports sells sports data as a live stream. Scouts and feeds capture what's happening across thousands of matches; TRADE360 is the product that delivers it to sportsbooks in real time.

The business problem: a sportsbook running in-play betting on 500 simultaneous matches cannot watch 500 matches. It buys a stream that says, within a second or two, that a goal went in — so it can suspend the market before someone watching a faster TV feed picks off the stale odds.

The one-line framing Latency is the product. The seconds between a goal and a suspended market are exactly where a sportsbook loses money, and that's what customers pay to close.

Your architecture

A MatchProducer replays a recorded World Cup match on the real match clock, publishing to a RabbitMQ topic exchange named trade with routing keys feed.<fixture_id> and markets.<fixture_id>. A TradeConsumer subscribes, rebuilds MatchState from the message stream, runs the in-play model every minute, and publishes 1X2 market updates.

The message contract

Every message is a TRADE360-style envelope: a Header carrying Type, MsgGuid, ServerTimestamp and a per-fixture monotonic MsgSeq, plus a Body. Five types, matching theirs:

TypeNameCarriesWhy it exists
1FixtureMetadataUpdateteams, competition, venue, kickoffthe customer needs to know what match this is
2LivescoreUpdatescore, minute, incidents, xGthe actual live state — the heart of the feed
3MarketUpdateprobability, fair price, book pricethe priced market the sportsbook trades on
31KeepAlivenothing — it's a heartbeatproves the connection is alive when the match is quiet
35Settlementthe deciding resultthe market is now officially decided

The three things that make it credible

MsgSeq — gap detection

A per-fixture counter. The consumer tracks the last sequence it applied; if it receives 47 when it expected 46, it knows it missed something. Without that number a dropped message is invisible and the scoreboard is silently wrong — which, on a betting feed, is money.

KeepAlive — liveness

Without heartbeats, "nothing has happened for 90 seconds" and "the connection died 90 seconds ago" look identical to a consumer. Type 31 makes silence detectable.

Snapshot recovery — the part that impresses

When a gap is detected, or a client joins mid-match, it cannot replay history: RabbitMQ deletes delivered messages. There is nothing to rewind. So the consumer makes an RPC call on snapshot.<fixture_id>; the producer — the authority for that fixture — replies with the full current MatchState. The consumer applies it, then discards any buffered message with MsgSeq <= LastMsgSeq, because the snapshot already covers it.

Why this matters more than anything else in the project This is not a pattern you invent by accident. It's precisely why LSports ships a Snapshot API, and it's the strongest single signal in your portfolio that you understand their problem domain rather than just their tech list. Files: tradepipe/snapshot.py, tradepipe/state.py (seq_gap(), from_snapshot()).

The 90-second walkthrough — memorize this shape "It's a working miniature of a sportsbook data feed, modelled on LSports' TRADE360. The xG model

The question it answers: a shot was taken. Before we know the outcome, what is the probability it becomes a goal? A tap-in might be 0.76; a 30-yard effort 0.03.

The four stages

  1. The shot becomes data — location, angle, distance, body part, assist type, defensive pressure, whether it was tagged a big chance.
  2. 23 features — the shot is reduced to exactly 23 facts the model is allowed to see. Nothing else. Your explainer lets a reader expand all 23 with real values.
  3. Three models, blended — the 23 features are scored by three separate models and averaged. Crucially: how much say each one gets was decided by testing, not by hand.
  4. Calibration — raw model outputs are not honest probabilities. Calibration maps them so that shots the model calls "30%" go in about 30% of the time.
The feature worth name-dropping big_x_dist. A "big chance" tag would otherwise apply at full strength from 6 metres or from 25 metres. This feature shaves the bonus back per metre, so the tag still respects distance instead of overriding it. That's a hand-designed interaction — evidence you thought about football, not just about scikit-learn.

The number that proves it works

Brier score 0.0722, measured on shots the model never trained on. Brier is mean squared error between predicted probability and what actually happened — 0 is perfect.

Always quote the baseline with it Goals happen on roughly 1 shot in 10. A model that guessed "10%" every single time, knowing nothing about anything, would already score about 0.09. So the benchmark isn't zero — it's 0.09. Getting to 0.0722 means the model is genuinely reading the shot.

Quoting a metric with no reference point is what a student does. Quoting the naive baseline alongside it is what an engineer does.

The methodological point to lead with

Your own page says it outright, and you should say it out loud:

Say this "Calibrating a model against its own training predictions is how false confidence survives review. Every calibration number on that page comes from shots held out of training."

That single sentence separates someone who got a good number from someone who knows why a good number might be a lie.

The xA extension

Same idea for passes, but two-stage: given this completed pass, (a) what is the probability a shot follows, and (b) how good would that shot be? Multiply the two, then calibrate the product. 19 pass features, roughly 2.4M passes.

Why two stages beat one black box: a pass can be dangerous by creating a chance that never materialises. Modelling "does a shot happen" and "how good is it" separately, then combining, is more honest — and it means you can inspect which half of the estimate is driving a result.

The win-prob model

The question it answers: given the score, the clock, and how the match is actually going — who wins? This is the model your pipeline runs live, and it drew the lines in your match centre.

Training data: 3,272 matches and 82,310 shots — four seasons of the EPL and La Liga, plus World Cups 2018, 2022 and 2026. Everything you have ever scraped, in one corpus.

Part A — the pre-match rate (Dixon-Coles-lite)

Before kickoff you need λ: expected goals for each side.

λ = att · opp_def / μ   (× hfa for the home side)

Four ideas make this good rather than naive:

  1. Each competition gets its own baseline μ. A team is measured against its own league's scoring environment, not "football in general." Your fitted values: EPL 1.4184, La Liga 1.2871, World Cup 1.3674. Pooling them would distort both.
  2. Team rates are shrunk toward that baseline (empirical Bayes). A team with 3 matches of evidence shouldn't be trusted like one with 100. The shrinkage strength k was tuned over {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} by holdout Brier — k = 1 won, meaning the data preferred rates that stay responsive.
  3. Teams are namespaced by competition. EPL/Arsenal and WC/Argentina are separate entities, so league form never leaks into a World Cup prior.
  4. Recency decay of 0.8 per season. Last season counts ×0.8, two seasons ago ×0.64. Teams change.

Plus home advantage ×1.1295, applied to league matches only and dropped to 1.0 at neutral World Cup venues.

Worked example — the match in your screenshot WC/Argentina: att 1.7298, def 0.7284 (19 matches). WC/Switzerland: att 1.5491, def 1.1608 (14 matches). μ(WC) = 1.3674, neutral venue so no hfa. That produces the pre-match rates the pipeline actually used: λ_home = 1.468, λ_away = 0.825 — a kickoff quote of 43.0% / 35.2% / 21.8%.

Part B — going live

This one formula drives the entire dashboard:

lam_rem(t) = ((90 − t) / 90) · [ (1 − w)·λ_pre + w·90·pace₁₅ ]
  • (90 − t)/90Time left. At minute 45 half the match remains, so halve the expected goals. At 90 it's zero — nothing more can happen.
  • λ_preWhat we expected before kickoff, from Part A.
  • pace₁₅What's happening right now — xG created in the last 15 minutes ÷ 15, giving xG per minute, then scaled back up to a full match.
  • w = 0.35The blend. 65% prior belief, 35% live momentum.

Then feed both teams' lam_rem plus the current score into an independent-Poisson grid, 0–10 goals per side, and bucket all 121 cells by final result:

P(home) = Σᵢⱼ Pois(i; λ_h) · Pois(j; λ_a) · [ h+i > a+j ]

Draw and away are the same sum with == and <. The triple is renormalized to exactly 1.0, because truncating the grid at 10 goals loses a sliver of tail mass.

Two guards worth mentioning: LAM_FLOOR = 0.05 means a dominated team is never completely dead, and ODDS_CAP = 999 stops near-zero probabilities from producing absurd prices.

Rehearsal

Reading is not the same as being able to say it. Answer each of these out loud before opening the answer.

"Walk me through your live pipeline project."

Use the 90-second script from section 2. Structure: what it imitates → the flow → the message contract → the failure handling → how it's tested. Do not start with the model; start with the architecture, because that's what a backend interviewer is asking about.

"Why do you need a snapshot API at all? Can't you just replay the messages?"

Because RabbitMQ is a mailbox, not a log — once a message is delivered and acked, it's gone. There is no history to replay. So a late joiner or a consumer that detected a gap requests current state instead, then resumes the live stream, discarding anything with MsgSeq <= LastMsgSeq. (Bonus: with Kafka you could replay the retained log — that trade-off is exactly why a company might run both.)

"How do you test a distributed system without the infrastructure?"

The broker is an interface with two implementations. InMemoryBroker is synchronous and deterministic — the producer and consumer run in one process, in a fixed order, so 18 tests run with no RabbitMQ, no Docker, no sleeps. RabbitBroker is the real transport, swapped in via a factory. Same code path either way.

"Your model's Brier is 0.6176. Isn't that terrible?"

It's a three-outcome Brier, so the scale runs 0–2, not 0–1 — different from the binary 0.0722 on the xG model. The number that matters is the comparison: a constant base-rate quote scores 0.6449 at every checkpoint, and the model beats it throughout, improving as the match progresses (0.6176 → 0.3652 by minute 75) as uncertainty resolves.

"What's wrong with your win-probability model?"

Answer honestly and specifically: the momentum term is not earning its place. w = 0 beats w = 0.35 at every live checkpoint in the blind holdout. It's kept because it makes the timeline more responsive to watch, but for real pricing I'd drop it. Also: independent Poisson ignores the known correlation between scorelines (the reason Dixon-Coles has a low-score correction), and there's no red-card or substitution term.

"Explain xG to someone who's never heard of it, in 20 seconds."

Every shot gets a probability of becoming a goal, based only on what was knowable at the moment it was struck — distance, angle, body part, pressure. A tap-in is 0.76, a 30-yarder is 0.03. Add them up and you get how many goals a team should have scored, which is a far better predictor of future results than the goals they actually scored.

"Why should we hire a student with no industry experience?"

Don't argue with the premise — redirect to evidence. You built a working miniature of their product before applying: their message contract, their snapshot-recovery pattern, their market pricing with an overround. Then the honest close: you're not claiming production experience, you're claiming you already understand the problem they solve and can be useful quickly.
